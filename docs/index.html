Counterfactual Regret Minimization

How a program learns to play poker perfectly

No opening book, no hand-coded rules, no human games to copy. It plays itself a few hundred thousand times and keeps score of the moves it wishes it had made. That turns out to be enough to reach a strategy nobody can beat.

Kuhn poker. A three-card deck. Each player antes 1 chip and gets one card. Player 1 acts first and may pass or bet 1 chip. Facing a bet you may call or fold; facing a pass the other player may pass (showdown) or bet. Highest card wins the pot at a showdown, and a fold hands the pot over without one.

Small enough to solve exactly on paper, which is exactly why it is the standard test case — you can check the program against the known answer. The whole game is five possible endings:

history ending net to the player who just acted pp both pass, showdown for 1 chip ±1 bp bet, fold +1 bb bet, call, showdown for 2 chips ±2 pbp pass, bet, fold +1 pbb pass, bet, call, showdown for 2 chips ±2

A player never sees the other card, so what they actually know is their own card plus the betting so far. That pair is called an information set. Three cards × four betting situations ("" · p · b · pb) gives this game exactly 12 information sets — 12 decisions to get right, and the entire job is picking a pass/bet mix for each one.

What CFR is

The obstacle

You cannot just play the highest-value move, because value depends on what the opponent does — and their best response depends on you. Chase it directly and you get a cycle: you adjust, they adjust, you adjust back. There is no fixed target to hill-climb toward.

What you want instead is a Nash equilibrium: a pair of strategies where neither player can improve by changing their own. CFR finds one without ever searching for it directly. It optimizes a different quantity that happens to drag the strategy to equilibrium as a side effect.

That quantity is regret

At one information set, for one action a, regret answers:

How much better off would I have been if I had always played a here, instead of what my strategy actually did?

Positive regret means the action is underused. So use it more — literally in proportion to its regret. That rule is regret matching:

σ(a) = R⁺(a) / Σa' R⁺(a') where R⁺ = max(R, 0) all regrets ≤ 0 → fall back to a uniform mix

Negative regret is discarded rather than inverted: "I wish I hadn't" is not an instruction to do the opposite, only to stop doing this. And notice what the denominator does — an action with twice the regret gets twice the probability, so the mix is always mixed. Nothing ever collapses to a single pure action while another still has positive regret. That is what makes bluffing appear on its own.

Two details that make it actually work

1. Measure against the baseline, not against zero. The value of an action is compared with what the current strategy is already worth at that node:

uσ = Σa σ(a)·u(a) what the current mix is worth here R(a) += u(a) − uσ regret, relative to that

This matters more than it looks. If two actions are worth 1.16 and 1.17, splitting by raw value gives 49.8% / 50.2% and the program never learns. Subtracting the baseline turns that thin edge into the entire signal. It also creates the fixed point: because uσ is an average of the action values, playing a high-regret action more pulls uσ toward it, which shrinks its own regret. The system settles where every action in use has equal value — which is precisely the indifference condition that defines the equilibrium. Zero regret and indifference are the same statement.

2. Weight by the opponent's chance of getting you here, not your own. This is the "counterfactual" in the name:

R(a) += πopp · (u(a) − uσ)

Suppose your current strategy never bets the Jack. Then you never reach any decision that follows a Jack bet. Weight regret by your own probability of arriving and those decisions count for nothing, so you never learn to fix them, so you keep not going there — a trap you cannot climb out of. Dropping your own reach probability breaks it: counter to fact, assume you got here, and evaluate honestly.

The running average of your strategy uses the opposite weight — your own reach. An iteration where you rarely reached a node should not dominate the record of what you do there. Two ledgers, two different weights; mixing them up is the classic implementation bug.

Why it converges

Regret matching has a guarantee: average regret per iteration falls like 1/√T, so it goes to zero. Bolt that to a folk theorem about two-player zero-sum games —

if both players' average regret is at most ε, their average strategies form a 2ε-Nash equilibrium

— and the algorithm follows. Drive regret to zero by self-play and the average strategy is driven to equilibrium for free. You never solve for the equilibrium; you just refuse to keep regretting things.

The average, not the latest. The current strategy oscillates forever and never settles — watch the bars below jitter while training. Only the reach-weighted average over all iterations converges. The plain unweighted average does not work: behavioural strategies do not average linearly.

The algorithm, in full

One recursive walk of the game tree. This is the whole thing — the Python in this repository is this plus bookkeeping.

cfr(history, π₁, π₂): if history is terminal: return payoff(history) — value to whoever acts here σ = R⁺ / ΣR⁺ — regret matching strategy_sum += πself · σ — own reach, for the average for a in {pass, bet}: u[a] = −cfr(history + a, π with σ(a) folded in) — negate: value changes sides uσ = Σ σ(a)·u[a] — the baseline for a in {pass, bet}: R[a] += πopp · (u[a] − uσ) — counterfactual regret return uσ
π₁, π₂
probability each player's strategy would have played the moves leading here
u[a]
value of taking action a and playing the current strategy afterwards
R[a]
running regret for a, accumulated over every iteration
−cfr(…)
every value is stated from the point of view of whoever is about to act, so it flips sign on the way back up

Deal a random hand, call it once, repeat. Nothing else. Chance is handled by sampling one deal per iteration, so the 1/6 probability of each deal is implicit in how often it comes up rather than multiplied in.

Is it actually converging?

Exploitability is the honest test: compute the best possible counter-strategy to what the program currently plays, and see how much it wins. At a true equilibrium no counter-strategy gains anything, so this hits zero. It is computed here by brute force over all 64 pure strategies per side — no estimation.

exploitability |game value − (−1/18)|

Both axes are logarithmic; the straight-line fall is the 1/√T rate the theorem promises. It starts at 0.92 chips per hand — a uniform random strategy is enormously exploitable — and 50,000 iterations bring it under 0.01.
